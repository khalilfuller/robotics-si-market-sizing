Robotics Systems Integration Market Sizing

October 2026 · Khalil Fuller

Bottom line and definitions

The robotics, warehouse and manufacturing SI market today is about $55–65B of integration value-add on ~$110–120B of integrated project value. As robots move into roughly ten new industries, it could reach ~$140–170B of value-add by 2035, about 2.6x today.

  • Integration value-add is the SI's own revenue pool: engineering, software, controls, installation, commissioning and lifecycle service. It excludes the robot and any hardware the integrator passes through. This is the headline metric.

  • Integrated project value is what customers pay for turnkey systems, including resold hardware or, for OEM-integrators such as Daifuku, hardware they build themselves.

  • Scope: global, US$, calendar 2025 for today and nominal 2035 for the potential. Included: robot cells, warehouse and intralogistics systems, and controls/OT integration. Excluded: enterprise IT integration and surgical robots, which are sold direct.

1. Bottoms-up: today (2025)

Adding up three segments gives ~$54B of value-add (range $41–69B) on ~$110B of project value (range $94–127B).

Largest SIs, and how much of their revenue is SI

Only about half of the big names report revenue that is cleanly integration work. Most are OEM-integrators that also sell hardware they build, or groups whose SI unit sits next to unrelated divisions. Daifuku is the biggest gap: the warehouse segment above takes its full $4.4B, but only ~$1.4B of that comes from warehouse-type customers.

How this feeds the sizing. The warehouse segment in Section 1 sums the top 11 at $24.2B, which takes Daifuku at its full ~$4.4B. On a strict warehouse-only reading, only ~$1.4B of Daifuku belongs there. That cuts the sum to ~$21.2B, warehouse value-add to ~$14B and the bottoms-up total to ~$52B. Vanderlande's airport business and SSI Schaefer's racking and plastics would cut further, but neither discloses a split.

Daifuku's other ~$2.2B is not lost. Its cleanroom and auto-plant revenue is real factory automation, already captured by the robot-cell and controls segments, so it belongs in a different row rather than added back. The net effect is to place the bottoms-up figure at the lower end of its $41–69B range rather than to move the headline.

3. Bottoms-up: potential (2035)

2035 value-add comes to ~$150B (range $112–196B). About $115B comes from today's three segments growing at their own published forecast rates, and about $35B from industries that barely buy robots today.

Method. Three rules govern every row:

  1. Core growth follows sourced forecasts. Each segment grows at its published forecast rate through that forecast's horizon, then at half the rate to 2035. The fade is my assumption, since hypergrowth rarely holds for a full decade.

  2. Every new-industry row is triangulated. A unit build (units × adoption × capex) is set against a third-party market forecast extrapolated to 2035 at its own CAGR. The lower of the two is used unless the forecast's definition matches the row, because most reports define their market more broadly than robots deployed on site.

  3. The SI share depends on how much integration the robot needs. The anchor is Unchained's cost split for a bespoke turnkey cell: engineering is 20–45% and install 10–25%. Three tiers follow:

    • A, bespoke cells (45%). The core segments' own assumption.

    • B, mobile fleets wired into a site's systems (30%) — elevators, EHR, SCADA.

    • C, drop-in products (20%).

    Tiers B and C are my judgment, scaled down from the bespoke split.

How each unit build is derived:

  • General-purpose / humanoid overlay: Goldman's 2035 humanoid TAM of $138B, halved so it does not double-count the rows below.

  • Clinical & life-science labs: lab automation of $9.2B (2025) → $20.7B (2034) → ~$22.7B in 2035 at the source's 9.4% CAGR.

  • Agriculture: ~5.7M farms over 50 ha, <1% of farms but ~75% of farmland, × 1% a year × $250k.

  • Hospitals & care: ~58k addressable of ~165k hospitals × 10% × $1.5M a program.

  • Construction: ~80M workers in China, the US, the EU, Japan and Korea (of ~220M across construction, materials and real estate) × 5 robots per 10k workers × $250k.

  • Restaurants: 2.1M chain units × 4% × $100k.

  • Energy & infrastructure: robotic solar install plus wind, grid and pipe inspection. My estimate; no single source covers it.

  • Data centers: 12,048 sites in 2035 × 15% × $2M.

  • Professional cleaning & security: ~30k units in 2025 growing 25%/yr × $30k. The base is my estimate at 12% of IFR's ~250k professional service robots; IFR gives the cleaning share but no unit count, and security is not included.

  • Hotels: 86k properties in CoStar's global sample × 10% × $150k.

Forecasts given only as a CAGR plus an increment were converted to a level. Construction comes to $1.6B in 2025, and hotels to $0.65B. Restaurants is the only row where the two methods land on the same number; the rest disagree by 1.1–33x, so the published forecasts are not a reliable anchor on their own.

  • Low, $112B: the core at 6% a year instead of its sourced rates, non-humanoid adoption halved, and Goldman's earlier $38B humanoid TAM.

  • High, $196B: every core forecast held for the full decade with no fade, non-humanoid adoption × 1.75, and Goldman's 2022 $152B blue-sky case.

  • Where the pool sits: about 77% of it comes from segments that exist today, and warehouse becomes the largest single pool at ~$45B. Growth is concentrated in scaling what already works, not in the new industries.

  • Industries left out: mining (Caterpillar and Komatsu integrate autonomy themselves), surgical robots (sold direct), retail micro-fulfillment (already in the warehouse number), sidewalk delivery, recycling, laundry and defense.

4. Top-down: potential (2035)

Sizing from the labor robots can technically replace gives ~$167B of value-add in 2035 (range $78–332B).

  1. World GDP 2025 is $118.2T (IMF). At a 52.4% labour share (ILO, 2024), that is $61.9T of labor income.

  2. Robots could technically automate ~13% of US work hours (McKinsey Global Institute, Agents, robots, and us). That makes $8.05T of robot-addressable labor today, or $11.9T in 2035 at 4% nominal growth a year.

  3. Calibration: today's ~$91B of robot-cell and warehouse project spend replaces about 0.56% of that pool each year, assuming capex equals about two years of the labor it saves.

  4. 2035: the automation rate rises to 1.5% of the pool a year (range 1.0–2.2%). At 2.0 years of capex per $1 of labor (range 1.5–2.5), robot capex is $357B. A 40% SI share (range 35–45%) gives $143B, and non-robot controls integration growing at 6% adds $24B, for $167B.

Cross-check: McKinsey expects robotics hardware to reach $450B by 2040: $80B industrial and $370B general-purpose. Assume 1.3x of value-add per $1 of industrial robots and 0.3x for general-purpose robots, which are designed to need less integration. That gives ~$215B of SI value-add in 2040, in line with $140–170B in 2035.

Reconciliation

The two methods agree within about 20% in 2025 and about 10% in 2035. Read the market as ~$60B of value-add today and ~$160B by 2035, growing about 10% a year.

  • Biggest swing: how much integration general-purpose robots still need. If humanoids arrive integration-light, the overlay shrinks toward zero and the 2035 bottoms-up loses its ~$14B. If they need site engineering the way today's cells do, it roughly doubles, adding about as much again.

  • Who keeps it: the value-add pool is not the same as the independent-SI pool. OEM-integrators (Daifuku, Dematic, Toyota, Symbotic) already take most warehouse integration dollars, and Symbotic's 2026 tuck-ins (Fox Robotics, ARMS Innovations) point to more vertical integration.

  • What would break the top-down: MGI's 13% is the technical potential of US work hours. Physical work is a larger share of hours globally but is paid less, and the real unknown is adoption pace: 0.56% a year today versus the 1.5% assumed for 2035.

What happens if it goes right? Integration becomes the toll road of the physical-AI economy. Robots take over the ~$11.9T of robot-addressable labor at 2–3% a year instead of 0.6%. Whoever turns deployment into a product (standard cells, AI-written controls, fleet service) earns a slice of every robot installed in every hospital, farm, kitchen and job site. That is a $300B+ annual pool by the mid-2030s with recurring service on top, and no company yet plays the Accenture or United Rentals role in it.

Confidence and limitations

Confirmed against the cited source: IFR's 2025 installs (600k+) and its 655k (2026) and 806k (2029) forecasts; IFR's $16.7B install value; world GDP of $118.2T (IMF WEO Apr 2026); warehouse automation of $30.0B growing 14% to 2031; industrial automation of $238B, $255.9B and $275B; SkyQuest's $25.2B SI market; Unchained's cost splits; ABI's 12,048 data centers; FAO farm shares (<1% of farms, ~75% of land); ~165k hospitals; 2.1M chain restaurants; McKinsey's $450B 2040 forecast ($80B industrial, $370B general-purpose); Daifuku ¥660.7B; KION Supply Chain Solutions €3.07B; and Honeywell WWS ~$935M.

The weakest inputs, worth knowing before leaning on any of this:

  • The 3x system multiple comes from an IFR study published in 2012, using 2011 data.

  • Technavio's >20% services share cites an unnamed study.

  • Other firms put industrial automation lower — Straits at $209B for 2025 — so the low case could go lower still.

  • The 80M-worker construction subset and the top-11 warehouse sum of $24.2B are plausible but could not be traced to a single published page.

  • The fade rule, the tier B and C SI shares and the 50% humanoid de-duplication are judgment calls, not sourced figures.

  • Several 2035 rows lean on market-research CAGRs, which tend to run high.

The cheapest way to tighten the estimate would be 5–10 conversations with integrators working in hospitals, labs and agriculture, to price what their services share actually is.
